# Framing and Motivation

Use this reference to build a persuasive argument without converting narrative preference into evidence.

## Separate epistemic status

Maintain an evidence ledger:

| Statement | Status | Basis | What would verify or refute it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Field fact | `VERIFIED` or `UNKNOWN` | Primary source/data | Independent evidence |
| Interpretation | `INFERRED` | Reasoning across facts | Competing interpretation |
| Research hypothesis | `PROVISIONAL` | Proposed mechanism | Designed test/falsifier |
| Framing preference | `PROVIDED` | Authorial intent | Not an empirical claim |

Never hide a framing preference inside the background as though it were settled knowledge.

## Build the motivation chain

Use this sequence:

1. Establish the consequential setting without inflated universal claims.
2. Name the current practice or dominant assumption.
3. Identify a concrete friction, failure, cost, or contradiction.
4. Explain the underlying property that makes the friction persist.
5. State the capability a successful solution must have.
6. Introduce the research move as a response, not as an unsupported triumph.

Reject motivations that depend only on “few studies exist,” “the topic is important,” or “existing methods are inadequate” without specifying consequences and causes.

## Record Framing Intent

```markdown
### Framing Intent
- Intended reader update:
- Current default view or practice:
- Position taken: extend / complement / revise / challenge
- Legitimate emphasis:
- Strongest counter-position:
- Evidence that would weaken our framing:
- Claims that must remain bounded:
- Distortions or omissions to avoid:
```

Use the intended reader update to choose argument order and emphasis. Keep retrieval, comparison, and counterevidence assessment neutral.

## Convert properties into challenges

Write each challenge as:

`setting property -> induced technical difficulty -> failure of generic approach -> required capability`

Example pattern:

> Sparse observations leave multiple solutions compatible with the input; point-estimate methods collapse this ambiguity; the solution therefore needs uncertainty-aware inference and evaluation.

Avoid labels such as “complex,” “dynamic,” or “challenging” unless the causal consequence is explained.

## Audit the story

Check whether:

- the motivation survives if the proposed method is removed;
- the gap follows from evidence rather than rhetorical contrast;
- prior work is described fairly under its own assumptions;
- the proposed capability directly answers the diagnosed cause;
- boundary conditions are stated before broad implications;
- a skeptical reader can identify what would change the conclusion.
